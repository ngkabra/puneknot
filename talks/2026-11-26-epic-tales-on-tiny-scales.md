Long before the tales of Rama were made into films and TV series, the Mughal Emperor Akbar
commissioned a glorious translation of the Ramayana and the Mahabharata into Persian, and had the
manuscript illustrated by the finest artists of his atelier. Soon, his mother, Hamida Banu, and the
prime minister, Abdur Rahim Khanikhanan also commissioned their own copies. While Akbar's personal
copy remains inaccessible due to a court order, we have some reproductions of his manuscript, and
dispersed folios of imperial and sub-imperial copies in collections around the world.

Lavishly illustrated, these were elaborate graphic novels of the 16th century, and a part of a
longer tradition of patronising epic stories that celebrated kingship and valour, such as the
Shahnameh (Persian Book of Kings) and the Hamzanama (the fantastic adventures of Amir Hamza). Epics
and other stories continued to be visualised over centuries.

We look closely at these folios, and ask various questions: How did artists visualise beloved
characters? How did complex narratives fit folios, and how mini are miniatures? How did ateliers
function? And there are political questions too: why did a Mughal emperor choose to illustrate
Hindu epics? We trace familiar stories as illustrated in the painting ateliers of Sultanate, Mughal,
Rajput, and Deccani courts, over three hundred years! We discuss styles, techniques of storytelling
and the role of translation in the early modern era. It promises to be a visual treat for festive
times!

## About the speaker

Aparna Andhare is an independent art historian, curator, and writer. She delights in examining the
ways a cosmopolitan world was reflected in early modern art and architecture.

Aparna was a curator at the City Palace, Jaipur, and used to be a television producer and reporter
before she changed tack to art history. She has studied at the School of Arts and Aesthetics, JNU
(MA, 2010-12), and at the University of Edinburgh (2016-17). Most recently, she curated 'Domestic
Delights: Silver at Home', an exhibition on silverware at Zapurza Museum of Art and Culture, Pune,
under the aegis of India Foundation for the Arts' Archives and Museums grant.

She lives in Pune, writes catalogue and research essays, regularly teaches courses around history
of art, and is devoted to her cocker spaniel, Loki.
